TV programme recommendation
Midterm | Gleb Alektorov | 24 October 2026
Predicting five preferred programmes from viewing intervals and the broadcast schedule. Implementation,
chronological evaluation and platform strategy.
Repository: https://github.com/Moonkato/llm4rec_midterm/tree/main/solutions/midterm
The implementation and final CSV are packaged for this repository; remote publication is performed by the repository
owner. Code, evidence and candidate CSVs are stored in solutions/midterm.
1. Task and data
The output is exactly five ordered, distinct, nonzero tv_show_id values for each of 1,260 template users. The forecast
covers weeks 31-42 (27 July to 19 October 2020). MAP@5 rewards relevant programmes near the top of the list. User
IDs are preserved as strings to avoid 64-bit precision loss.
The supplied data contains 5,821,987 viewing intervals and three EPG schedule files: 1,074,235, 1,074,394 and
1,295,378 rows. A viewing duration is session length, not programme completion. The median raw session lasts 396
seconds, while extreme sessions can span days. Only 10.8% of schedule rows in weeks 11-20 carry a programme ID;
this percentage describes rows, not duration-weighted airtime coverage.
A session [s,e) is matched to an airing [a,b) only on the same channel, with overlap max(0, min(e,b)-max(s,a)). Airing
duration D=b-a provides the denominator. All historical features stop at the relevant cutoff. Interval timestamps use the
repository clock conversion, including its March 29 one-hour adjustment; replay checks cover missing-hour and
midnight cases.
Initially, an airing was watched if merged overlapping fragments covered at least 180 seconds. This filters brief channel
switches relative to the observed session median, but does not account for programme length. The later completion-
oriented protocol uses more than 30% of an airing for history and more than 80% for target counts, using a single
overlapping fragment rather than the union of interrupted fragments. The legacy target takes the maximum stored
overlap fragment; fragments separated by the artificial May 18 preprocessing boundary are not rejoined. Replay
features instead use raw continuous sessions. This is an operational approximation to preferred programmes, not a
verified reproduction of the hidden Kaggle label generator.
ID 0 is excluded from every item list and target. Its sessions can still describe channel/time viewing context. Future
EPG supplies programme availability, channel, start time and duration only; no test-period views enter training or
features. Metadata for the content model comes from the historical scope.
2. Evaluation contract
The main holdout is 18 May-27 July 2020; histories end on 18 May. The later robustness window is 22 June-27 July,
with history ending on 22 June. These windows overlap and are not independent test sets. Supervised rankers use an
earlier fit task: history 9 March-13 April and labels 13 April-18 May. Repeated validation-guided experimentation
creates selection optimism; the later window reduces but does not eliminate it.
For user u, AP@5 = sum over ranks r=1..5 of Precision@r times relevance(r), divided by min(5, number of relevant
items). Users with empty truth receive zero. MAP@5 averages all 1,260 users. The initial target is all distinct qualifying
180-second items; the later target selects the five most frequent qualifying >80% airings, with numeric-ID tie breaking.
These are different metrics even though both are called MAP@5.

3. Implemented methods and common output rules
Every system permits repeats of previously watched programmes and restricts output to nonzero programmes airing
during the forecast window. Cold users receive historical unique-viewer popularity restricted to eligible items. Zero-
signal or short lists are completed by that common fallback without duplicates. Ties use ascending numeric programme
ID; final rankings are truncated to five. The fallback rule is shared, but the number of cold users can change with the
historical qualification threshold.
Content-based filtering
Historical item attributes are tokenised into category, genre, director, actor and year/decade blocks. IDF is log((1+N)/
(1+df))+1. Each block is L2-normalised and weighted by 1.0, 1.5, 0.75, 1.0 and 0.35 respectively; the concatenated
item vector is normalised again. A user profile is the normalised sum of item vectors weighted by log(1+historical airing
count). Cosine similarity produces a ranking, not a viewing probability. Missing metadata gives a zero vector; there are
no title or identity tokens. The observed vocabulary has 11,939 features across the five blocks.
Implicit-feedback matrix factorization
ALS minimises sum c_ui(p_ui-x_u dot y_i)^2 plus lambda times the squared factor norms, with p_ui=1 for a positive
count and c_ui=1+alpha*log(1+count_ui). Missing pairs have preference zero and confidence one; they are not treated
as explicit dislikes. Alternating normal equations are solved in float64, and stored factors use float32. Dot products
rank eligible programmes.
The historical matrix contains 4,851 users, 8,233 items and 651,903 nonzeros. Tested (factors, alpha) pairs were
(32,1), (32,10), (64,10), (64,40); regularization=1, iterations=10, seed=20261022. The best initial ALS variant was 32
factors, alpha=1. No validation labels enter factor fitting.
Popularity and personal frequency
Popularity ranks by distinct historical viewers. Personal frequency ranks each user's historical qualifying airing counts
and uses popularity to fill zero-signal slots. Popularity is a required baseline and personal frequency is an additional
heuristic; neither is counted as one of the two required methods. Content filtering and implicit ALS satisfy that
requirement.

4. Schedule-aware ranking and final strategy
The weekly ranker v3 combines 19 history/popularity/channel/hour/availability features with five weekly-context
features: expected minutes, expected airing fraction, recent-28-day minutes, activity frequency and a recent/all-history
ratio. Candidate retrieval combines personal candidates (up to 100), popularity (30) and channel-hour candidates (30),
then deduplicates. This shortlist is a system component, not the entire eligible EPG catalogue.
LightGBM LambdaRank uses 417 trees, 15 leaves, maximum depth 4, minimum child count 100, L2=10, learning rate
0.03, label gains [0,1], truncation 10 and seed 20261023. The configuration and 417-tree count were selected from
three configurations on earlier inner-fit/inner-stop temporal tasks with early-stopping patience 100, then refitted on the
April 13-May 18 task. Training retains queries with both relevant and nonrelevant candidates. It learns ordering against
the top-five completion-count target. All fitted feature transforms and the model are frozen before each evaluation
forecast.
The airing model scores individual candidate airings from 33 features: the history/context base plus channel, weekday,
hour, duration, availability and session-coverage features. A binary LightGBM with 300 trees, 15 leaves, learning rate
0.05, minimum leaf count 100, L2=10 and max_bin=63 and seed=20261023 is trained on 473,908 sampled rows
(102,907 positive; 371,001 negative). Negative sampling probability is 0.01, with inverse-sampling weights 100 for
negatives and 1 for positives. Summed per-airing predictions form an expected-count ranking; they are not calibrated
satisfaction probabilities.
Hybrid v4 combines v3 and the airing model with equal reciprocal-rank fusion: score(i)=0.5/(60+rank_v3(i))+0.5/
(60+rank_event(i)). Rank fusion avoids pretending that a LambdaRank score and an expected count share a
probability scale. Equal weights and the RRF constant 60 were prespecified in the airing-model protocol, rather than
optimized on the final holdout. This fixed fusion improved both chronological windows relative to either component. It
was the strongest candidate among the first four submissions; the fifth submission subsequently improved both
returned scores.

5. Final experiment, submission evidence and limitations
The final experiment added categorical user/programme IDs to the original 24-feature ranker (26 features), and exact
historical same-weekday/channel session-replay features to the original 33-feature airing model (37 features).
Encoders were fitted only on training rows; unknown categories receive -1. Replay features measure >30% and >80%
single-session overlap, a recent-28-day completion rate and observable historical-date count. Sessions are clipped at
the cutoff; interrupted fragments are never merged into a synthetic continuous session.
Two fits completed in 791.59 seconds with 1.026 GB peak RSS. The combined identity/replay arm reached 0.419222
on the main holdout and 0.364225 on the later window, versus v4 at 0.417238 and 0.365450. Main delta +0.001984
has paired 95% interval [-0.001883, 0.005796]; later delta -0.001225 has interval [-0.005427, 0.002919]. It failed the
prespecified consistency screen. The combined CSV was submitted for the final external check. Kaggle returned
Private 0.44559 and Public 0.45546, both higher than v4. The screenshot below confirms the outcome; it does not
erase the earlier mixed validation evidence.
Earlier attempts included expanded weekly candidates, EASE features, rolling and full-history training, recurrence
features, alternative objectives and user-fold out-of-fold stacking. None established a clear, consistent replacement for
v4. LightFM installation failed under Python 3.13 and no LightFM training result is claimed. No external or synthetic
viewing records were added. Stopping avoids spending more compute on small, uncertain offline gains.

7. Platform choice and interpretation
I designate v5 (identity ranker plus replay airing model, equal RRF60) as the final competition strategy because the
completed external check improved both Private and Public MAP@5 over v4. V4 remains the stronger reference on
the later local window. For a platform, v5 is the candidate I would take forward to an online evaluation, with popularity
fallback retained; the small offline gains and mixed local evidence do not establish an online benefit or statistical
superiority on hidden labels.
For user 15634861151839696048, the historical profile includes news programmes such as Facts and TSN. Popularity
achieves AP@5=0.40, ALS 0.55, the weekly ranker 0.76 and v4 0.80. The schedule-aware system largely recovers
recurring preferences. This is useful familiarity, not evidence of discovery or enjoyment. Content filtering scores zero
despite descriptive attributes: semantic resemblance alone does not recover this user's five most frequent future
programmes.
For user 10269329346053634491, more history does not make the prediction easy. Popularity reaches 0.18, content
and ALS zero, personal frequency 0.10, and v4 0.20. The actual top five contain programmes missed by the models.
The contrast supports a failure case involving changing or insufficiently represented preferences; it does not establish
that all heavy viewers are difficult. The complete ordered IDs and observed hits above make this judgment inspectable.
ALS shares statistical strength through low-rank co-preference patterns, but this task rewards repeated schedules and
strongly time-dependent availability. Content features can connect related metadata where co-view data is thin, yet
missing or generic attributes and absent historical vectors for new items limit that benefit here. Association rules would
express direct conditional co-occurrence; graph diffusion would spread evidence through multiple co-view links. Those
mechanisms differ from ALS, but no unreported association/PPR score is used to justify the final choice.
Popularity already captures broadly watched news and is extremely cheap. It cannot express the user's programme-
specific weekday routine. The hybrid costs more, particularly when evaluating millions of airings, but runs at this scale
within about 1 GB and minutes rather than requiring a GPU. Candidate caching and collapsed repeated airing patterns
reduce inference cost. V5 export took 399.66 seconds, peaked at 838 MB and scored 8,297,845 replay tuples without
new fits.
Coverage is the primary limitation: most EPG rows lack item IDs, so both learning and evaluation ignore much of what
people watch. Non-viewed programmes are not proven dislikes, long sessions may be passive background viewing,
and device/channel activity does not reveal enjoyment. Completion counts overweight repeated short programmes and
changing broadcast supply. Only three test users are cold under the final export protocol, but sparse-history and new-
programme weaknesses remain.
No live A/B test, revenue lift, retention effect or causal business outcome was measured. An operational decision
should preserve repeat recommendations where supported by behaviour and evaluate discovery separately; unfamiliar
output is not automatically a better recommendation. The available evidence supports choosing the hybrid for this
dataset, not a universal ranking of recommendation families.
